# Use-case intake, screening and business case

## The problem
Proposals for new automation reach the architecture function through whatever channel the
submitter happens to use — a Teams message, an email, a slide in a steering deck, occasionally a
conversation in a corridor. They arrive in wildly different shapes: some name a business outcome
and no system, others name a product and no problem.

Before any of them can be compared with the others, someone has to establish the same facts for
each: what the problem actually is and who has it, which business capabilities it touches, how
critical failure would be, what it would cost to build and run, and what it would save. That work
is done by hand, by four different roles, and takes between three and six weeks per proposal. By
the time it is finished the sponsor has often moved on, and because each assessment is assembled
independently, two proposals scored by different people are not comparable.

Nothing records which version of the criteria an assessment was made against, so when the
capability map or the price list is revised, no one can say which past assessments are still valid.

## Who has it
Business owners submitting proposals wait weeks for an answer. The architecture function — a
business analyst, a solution architect, a risk officer and a cost engineer — carries the
assessment load. The Chief Architect answers for the portfolio decisions that rest on it, and for
the fact that today they rest on assessments that are not comparable.

## What changes if this works
Every proposal is established against the same criteria in hours rather than weeks, and each
assessment records the exact version of every reference it read, so a later revision makes the
affected assessments visible instead of silently stale. A human confirms the criticality class and
authorises the investment — those are judgements with accountability attached and they stay with
people. What stops being manual is the establishing of facts, not the deciding.

Proposals become comparable, because they were assessed against the same pinned criteria rather
than four people's recollection of them.

## What we already know
- Submissions carry personal data only incidentally (the submitter's own name) and no health data.
- The reference criteria — capability map, component catalogue, price list — are published and
  versioned centrally.
- An assessment that is wrong and confident is worse than one that says it does not know: a
  proposal rejected on a fabricated finding is not appealable, because nobody can see what it
  rested on.
- Required by the start of the next planning cycle.
